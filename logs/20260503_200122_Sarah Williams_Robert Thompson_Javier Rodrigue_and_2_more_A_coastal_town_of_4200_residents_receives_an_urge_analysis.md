# Simulation Analysis Report

**Premise:** A coastal town of 4,200 residents receives an urgent flood warning:
a Category 3 storm surge is expected within 12 hours, with water levels
projected at 8 feet above normal high tide. The National Weather Service
has upgraded the warning twice in the past 6 hours, and county authorities
have issued a mandatory evacuation order effective immediately.

However, institutional trust is severely compromised. In the past 3 years,
the town has experienced 2 false evacuation orders, and a 2024 FEMA audit
criticized the county's emergency communication infrastructure. Trust in
government warnings stands at 38% according to a recent community survey.

The town's 3 emergency shelters can accommodate approximately 2,500 people
(60% of the population). The single evacuation highway (Route 17) is
already at 70% capacity. Cell tower coverage is intermittent due to
preliminary storm bands. Social networks, community leadership, and
informal communication channels will determine who reaches safety in time.

Stakes: Based on storm surge modeling, residents who remain in flood zones
face a 35% probability of life-threatening conditions. The 12-hour window
is shrinking as the storm accelerates.

## Executive Summary

The simulation modeled an urgent Category 3 storm-surge evacuation in a coastal town of 4,200 residents, with an 8-foot surge expected within 12 hours, limited shelter capacity for about 2,500 people, Route 17 already at 70% capacity, intermittent cell service, and low institutional trust after prior false alarms and a FEMA audit. The five agents represented different roles in this stressed communication environment: Sarah Williams sought 90% evacuation compliance and zero fatalities among vulnerable residents; Robert Thompson aimed to evacuate his own household and confirm plans for at least three neighbors; Javier Rodriguez aimed to make an informed evacuation decision based on three independent sources; Eleanor O’Brien needed assisted transportation to an accessible shelter with medications and equipment; and Pastor Moses aimed to account for 35 vulnerable congregants, organize eight carpool vehicles, and open the church as a secondary gathering point.

The key turning point came immediately in Step 1, when Pastor Moses used the church fellowship hall as a trusted community coordination point and explicitly endorsed the county evacuation order despite residents’ unease. Steps 5–6 further shifted the response from ad hoc concern to organized logistics: Eleanor created a structured pickup list, and Pastor Moses formally made her the coordination hub. In Step 7, Sarah identified Marsh Street as the most urgent route because water was already pooling near the bait shop intersection, and in Steps 8 and 13 Javier drove that route and entered the Rivas house after a child was heard crying. Meanwhile, Robert joined Ben on the Bayberry Lane route in Steps 9 and 14, calming a nervous driver and helping navigate flooded roads and debris. New urgent information surfaced in Step 10, when Eleanor marked Harbor View Apartments as time-critical and added a Pelican Lane welfare check; Step 11 attempted to reroute teams, but by Step 15 those assignments were still delayed, prompting Eleanor to send Carl Jenkins’s high-clearance pickup toward Harbor View.

Goal attainment was mostly partial or unproven. Pastor Moses partially achieved his goal: he opened the church as an operational gathering point and organized volunteers, but the log does not confirm that all 35 vulnerable congregants were accounted for or that eight carpool vehicles were assembled. Sarah partially achieved her coordination goal by joining the east-side pickup team, prioritizing Marsh Street, and triaging urgent households, but there is no evidence of 90% townwide evacuation compliance or zero fatalities among vulnerable populations. Robert did not achieve his stated personal goal in the visible log: he helped with Bayberry Lane and intended to check on Mr. Bell and neighbors, but there is no confirmation that his own household evacuated within two hours or that three neighboring households had transportation plans. Javier did not achieve his stated decision-making goal: he did not gather three independent information sources, his call to his mother failed, and there is no evidence that he secured his property; instead, he shifted into rescue support. Eleanor also did not achieve her personal goal as written, because the log does not show her securing assisted transportation, medications, or medical equipment for herself; however, she substantially advanced the collective evacuation by managing the coordination list and dispatching resources.

The strongest emergent dynamic was the replacement of distrusted official communication with trusted local infrastructure: the church, Pastor Moses, Ruth’s phone table, Eleanor’s notepad, and volunteer vehicles became the de facto emergency operations network. Several agents subordinated their individual goals to collective rescue work: Javier set aside his own information-gathering and concern for his mother, Eleanor prioritized others’ transportation needs over her own evacuation, and Robert delayed his household-focused objective to support a nervous driver and vulnerable neighbors. Psychological components were visible in limited but plausible ways: Pastor Moses’s values appeared in his steady moral leadership; Sarah’s planned-behavior orientation appeared in route prioritization and resource coordination; Javier’s skepticism/cognitive-bias setup was suggested by his initial need for independent information, though his later behavior became action-oriented; Eleanor’s emotional component did not manifest as panic but as focused caregiving under pressure. No betrayals occurred; the main friction was operational deadlock and delay, especially around Harbor View and Pelican Lane, where Step 11’s rerouting plan was overtaken by field realities and required Eleanor’s improvised Step 15 solution.

Several agents used neighborhood familiarity as a decision resource.

This is important for researchers studying evacuation behavior: in low-trust environments, local knowledge and socially embedded actors may substitute for formal command-and-control capacity.

### Some agents acted as planners, others as field responders
A division of labor emerged:
