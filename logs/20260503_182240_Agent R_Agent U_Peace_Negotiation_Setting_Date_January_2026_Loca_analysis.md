## Executive Summary

This Concordia run simulated a high-stakes Russia–Ukraine peace negotiation in Istanbul in January 2026, mediated by a UN Game Master under a 48-hour deadline tied to a pending UN Security Council vote and the risk of renewed military escalation within 72 hours. Agent R’s goal was to secure written agreement on at least three key issues, specifically including recognition of Crimea, a binding Ukrainian neutrality commitment, and sanctions relief within 12 months. Agent U’s goal was to secure written agreement on at least four key issues, including a binding withdrawal timeline from occupied territories, reparations exceeding $50 billion, and preservation of the right to pursue EU membership. The stakes were framed as existentially costly for both sides: failure would mean renewed escalation and further sanctions pressure.

The negotiation began with firm red lines. In Step 1, Agent R opened by emphasizing the 48-hour need for written agreements and referenced overnight ceasefire reports as a technical matter. In Step 2, Agent U immediately rejected territorial status and “binding neutrality” as starting points, stating that Crimea was non-negotiable. Step 3 marked Agent R’s first attempt to reframe the agenda around verification and mutual core concerns, while Step 5 was an escalation point: Agent R rejected Agent U’s paper as “capitulation masquerading as a framework,” objecting to unconditional withdrawal and a large reparations demand. The main turning point came in Steps 7–10, when the parties moved from maximalist positions toward mechanisms: Agent R acknowledged parallel monitoring tied to sanctions relief and an EU preamble in Step 7; Agent U acknowledged movement on escrow, monitoring triggers, and the EU preamble in Step 8; Agent R then proposed three conditions in Step 9, including a frozen-conflict formulation for Crimea and neutrality effective on signature. In Step 10, a Turkish guarantor proposal became the key bridge, allowing neutrality to be anchored by an external actor “neither NATO nor Russia.”

Goal attainment was asymmetric and partly ambiguous. Agent R achieved a signed written agreement in Step 11 and explicitly stated that it secured “three of the core issues”: a binding neutrality structure, sanctions relief linked to verified withdrawal benchmarks on a twelve-month timeline, and Crimea language acknowledging de facto reality for the working group. However, Agent R did not obtain full legal recognition of Crimea; the final text was described as “not recognition in the strict legal sense,” so this part of the goal was only partially achieved. Agent U appears to have achieved partial success: the final agreement included verified withdrawal benchmarks and preserved some EU-related language through the “EU preamble” referenced in Steps 7–8, but the log does not explicitly confirm a binding withdrawal timeline from all occupied territories or a reparations framework exceeding $50 billion. Reparations were discussed earlier, and an escrow mechanism is referenced in Step 8, but the final outcome does not state an amount or confirm that it exceeded $50 billion.

The most notable emergent dynamic was a shift from positional bargaining to procedural and verification-based compromise. Both agents began with rigid red lines, but the interaction produced creative ambiguity: Crimea was handled through de facto administrative-control language rather than legal recognition, neutrality was made binding but externally guaranteed by Turkey, and sanctions relief was tied to monitored withdrawal benchmarks. The physical and emotional choreography—silences, sliding papers back and forth, setting pens down, and keeping voices calm—suggested controlled brinkmanship rather than impulsive escalation. The configured values and emotion components were visible mainly through restraint, resolve, and symbolic signaling: Agent U repeatedly used firm posture and direct rejection to defend sovereignty-related values, while Agent R maintained deliberate calm and treated recognition, neutrality, and sanctions relief as core strategic requirements. No alliances or betrayals emerged; instead, the decisive innovation came from third-party mediation and a mutually acceptable guarantor structure.

> “The neutrality commitment… is binding in structure…”

This suggests the agents reasoned through trade-offs rather than simple yes/no concessions.
